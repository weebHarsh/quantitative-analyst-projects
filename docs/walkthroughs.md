# Walkthrough scripts

Spoken scripts for each project: a 2-minute version (about 300 words) and a 10-minute version (about 1,000 to 1,300 words, at a relaxed speaking pace), then 6 likely questions with short honest answers. Closing sections cover the whole-portfolio story and questions to ask the interviewer.

Rules for every script:
- Say "synthetic" early. All data in all four projects is generated by the repo; no real market or live trading was involved (docs/decision-log.md D04, D25; roadmap rule 6).
- Numbers are quoted from the files named in parentheses. If you re-run something and a number moves, say the new number and update this file.
- Say "I" about decisions you made and "the review" about what the independent reviewer found. Do not claim work experience this repo does not show.
- Job-description wording is secondhand (docs/firm-landscape.md): it comes from job-board snippets, not the employer's own posting.

Pointers in parentheses are to files in this repo or to D-numbers in docs/decision-log.md.

# Project 1: Post-trade analytics engine

## 2-minute script

"This project answers two questions about a market-making strategy: where did the PnL come from, and was the edge real? It takes fills and quotes and splits mark-to-market PnL into spread capture, inventory and fees. (README, Method)

All the data is synthetic. A seeded simulator makes a price path with a short-lived order-flow signal, and a deliberately simple market maker produces fills. The strategy is not meant to make money. It exists to feed the analytics. (src/pta/data/synthetic.py, strategy/market_maker.py; D04)

On the sample run, 15,895 fills over six simulated hours, spread capture is plus 188.94, inventory is minus 151.95, fees are minus 31.58, and the total is plus 5.42. (examples/sample_report.md) The split is enforced by a test that computes equity a second, independent way, as cash plus position times mid, and a third way on the time grid. All three must agree. (D05, tests/test_pnl_identity.py)

Second, markouts: the average move of the mid after each fill, weighted by quantity. At zero seconds it is plus 0.598 basis points, which is the spread captured against mid. By one second it is 0.371, by five seconds 0.111, and by sixty seconds 0.021. That decay is adverse selection. (README, Headline results)

Third, honesty about significance. The annualised Sharpe is 2.62, but the t-statistic of the mean bar PnL is 0.16. Six hours of data scaled to a year looks impressive and means nothing. The report says so. (D08)

There is also a C++17 markout kernel that must agree with the Python to 1e-9, and 16 tests in total. (docs/work-log.md, roadmap section 3)

So the takeaway: I can build the attribution a desk asks for, show the evidence for and against edge, and say when a number is not significant."

## 10-minute script

**Opening (about 1 minute).** "Post-trade analytics is where a desk checks whether what it thought it was doing is what happened. I built a small engine for that, ran it on synthetic data, and I will walk through the problem, the three analyses, the bugs a review found, and the limits. The key sentence up front: the data is synthetic, the strategy is a fill generator, and I claim no trading edge. (README, Headline results; D04)"

**The inputs (about 1 minute).** "Two tables. Fills have a timestamp, side, price, quantity and fee. Quotes have a timestamp, bid, ask and mid. Timestamps are int64 microseconds, not float seconds, because with floats a grid time computed two ways can differ in the last bit and move an as-of lookup by one row. (src/pta/data/loaders.py; D06) The mid at time t is the last quote at or before t, so there is no look-ahead. (D07)"

**Attribution (about 2.5 minutes).** "For each fill, with side s, quantity q, price px, mid at the fill m and final mid M: s·q·(M minus px) equals s·q·(m minus px) plus s·q·(M minus m). The first term is spread capture, the edge against mid at the fill. The second is inventory, the price move while you hold the position. Net of fees, total equals spread capture plus inventory minus fees. (README, Method; D05)

Why that split? It is the question a trader asks: did I make money by quoting well, or by holding a position that happened to move? On the sample run spread capture is plus 188.94 and inventory is minus 151.95. The market maker earned the spread and gave most of it back to price moves against its inventory, which is what adverse selection looks like in aggregate. Fees are minus 31.58 and the total is plus 5.42 on 15,895 fills, which is plus 0.017 basis points of traded notional. (examples/sample_report.md)

The identity is not just asserted. A separate function computes the equity curve as cash plus position times mid, without using the decomposition, and the tests require the two to agree. (src/pta/pnl.py, tests/test_pnl_identity.py)"

**Markouts (about 2 minutes).** "A markout is side times the mid at fill-time plus horizon, minus the fill price, per unit. Positive means the market moved in our favour after the fill. Horizons run from 0 to 60 seconds, quantity-weighted, in basis points of price. At 0 seconds it is the captured spread, plus 0.598. Then 0.371 at one second, 0.111 at five, 0.021 at sixty. The share of fills with a positive markout falls from 100 percent at zero to about 60 percent at one second and about 50 percent at five. So within a few seconds the edge against mid is mostly gone. The simulator builds that in on purpose: the order-flow signal moves price and also skews which side aggressors hit. (README; D07, src/pta/markouts.py)

A fill whose time plus horizon is past the last quote has no markout and is dropped, not extrapolated. That is why the sample count falls at long horizons."

**Risk and significance (about 1.5 minutes).** "On one-minute bars I compute max drawdown (31.27 on the sample), historical VaR and expected shortfall at 95 and 99 percent, Sharpe, Sortino, and the t-statistic. The Sharpe is 2.62 annualised, assuming 390 bars a day and 252 days. The t-statistic is 0.16. A t-statistic under about 2 says the mean is not distinguishable from zero. The first smoke run printed a Sharpe of 2.6 on a near-zero mean, and that is why the t-statistic sits next to it in the report. (src/pta/risk.py; D08)"

**The C++ kernel (about 45 seconds).** "A C++17 two-pointer kernel does the attribution and markouts in O(n plus m). It is tested against Python to 1e-9. I timed it afterwards and it is not faster: about 123 ms against 11 ms in Python on 15,895 fills and 216,000 quotes, because the command-line kernel spends its time parsing CSV while the Python works in memory with vectorised lookups. So it is a cross-check, not an optimisation. The point of the exercise was a second implementation that agrees, which catches errors in both. (decision D47) (cpp/markout_kernel.cpp, tests/test_cpp_parity.py)"

**What the review found (about 45 seconds).** "An independent reviewer found two loader bugs and reproduced both: float64 cannot hold epoch nanoseconds exactly, so quotes shifted and merged; and an integer cast truncated a quantity of 1.5 to 1. Both fixed with tests. Now nanoseconds use integer division and quantities stay floats. (D18, tests/test_loaders.py)"

**Limits and close (about 30 seconds).** "Synthetic data only. Single instrument. No queue position or latency, which is what Project 4 addresses. The real-quotes adapter has not been run against a real feed. I would not say any of this shows edge. It shows I can build and defend the analysis. (README, Limitations)"

## Questions

**1. Is this a real edge?** No. The data is simulated, the strategy is a fill generator, and the t-statistic is 0.16, so the total of plus 5.42 is indistinguishable from zero. The project shows the tooling. (README; D04, D08)

**2. What would you do with real data?** First check the quote and fill timestamps share a clock and units, since `market_from_quotes` handles ns, us and ms but no vendor layout has been verified. Then run attribution and markouts on one day, compare against the desk's own PnL, and expect the identity to expose any timestamp or fee-convention mismatch. (README, Data schemas and Limitations)

**3. What did the review find?** Two loader bugs: epoch nanoseconds lost precision in float64, and a quantity of 1.5 truncated to 1. I fixed both and added tests. (D18)

**4. What would you do differently?** Decide what the C++ kernel is for before writing it: I assumed it would be faster, then timed it and it was about ten times slower end to end, so I now describe it as a cross-check (D47). Add a queue and latency model earlier (that became Project 4). Run on one real day before calling the adapter done. (README, Limitations)

**5. Why is spread capture positive at 0 s but the total near zero?** Spread capture is measured against the mid at the fill; inventory PnL is the move after. The simulator builds in adverse selection, so most of the captured spread is given back (+188.94 vs -151.95). (examples/sample_report.md)

**6. Why does the Sharpe of 2.62 not impress you?** It scales six hours to a year, assuming 390 bars a day and 252 days; the t-statistic of the mean is 0.16. A few hours of data cannot support a Sharpe claim. (D08, docs/learning/t-statistic-vs-sharpe.md)

# Project 2: Real-time risk and strategy monitor

## 2-minute script

"This is what a trader or risk manager would watch during a session. It replays fills and quotes, keeps PnL, inventory, drawdown and markouts up to date in constant time per event, and raises alerts against limits. The data is synthetic, so it shows monitoring, not edge. (README)

The first design goal was that the incremental numbers equal Project 1's batch numbers. A test checks the attribution split, equity at every quote, max drawdown and markouts. An independent reviewer ran it on a fresh seed and it matched every one of 72,000 equity ticks. (tests/test_equivalence.py, README, How it was checked)

Four alert rules: position, loss from peak, fill rate and markout degradation. Each has hysteresis, so it clears at a safer level than it fires, plus a cooldown, a warm-up and a minimum sample count. The thresholds come from 20 clean two-hour sessions plus one six-hour session, with the margin over the worst clean value stated. (config/limits.toml, D14, D15)

To test it I built a bad session from five simulated phases: normal, adverse selection, an inventory run, a fill drought, recovery. It raises 9 alerts in the order the phases would predict, and the clean session raises none. (examples/demo_bad.txt, D16)

It has 123 tests. A review found five bugs in the alert layer, for example a higher-severity alert hidden by the cooldown after a clear, and all were fixed with regression tests. (D14b, D17) The full engine handles about 110,000 events per second, against roughly 10 quotes per second in a live session. (D26)

Limits: thresholds fit this simulator only and would need recalibrating on real flow. The server is localhost-only with no authentication. (D15, D20)"

## 10-minute script

**Opening (about 45 seconds).** "Project 1 is batch: you analyse after the session. A monitor is the live version. The question is: can I get the same numbers incrementally, and can I design alerts that fire on real problems without crying wolf? The data is synthetic, and I am showing monitoring, not a trading edge. (README)"

**Replay and state (about 2 minutes).** "The replay merges Project 1's quotes and fills into one ordered stream. At the same timestamp the quote goes first, because in Project 1 a fill stamped on a quote uses that quote's mid, and the incremental code must see the same order. (src/rmon/replay.py; D11) The state object keeps cash, fees, position, attribution, equity, peak and drawdown, and a tracker resolves each fill's markouts as quotes arrive. Each event costs constant time amortised. (src/rmon/state.py, markouts_stream.py)

Two semantic decisions made the batch match exact. Inventory is marked to the current mid, since the final mid is unknown live; at the end of the session the total equals Project 1's. (D10) Drawdown is measured once per distinct timestamp, so the transient dip between a quote and fills sharing its timestamp is not counted as a loss. (D12) The test suite has 15 equivalence tests, and the independent run matched 72,000 ticks. (tests/test_equivalence.py)"

**Alert design (about 3 minutes).** "Four rules. Position: warn at 60, breach at 100. Loss from peak: warn 100, breach 200. Fill rate: breach below 6 fills in a 60-second window, or above 150. Markout degradation: rolling one-second markout below minus 0.75 basis points, with at least 50 samples. (config/limits.toml, README Alert design)

Hysteresis: a rule clears only after the metric has recovered past a safer level, for example position warns at 60 and clears below 40. Cooldown: repeats of the same or lower severity are damped, but a higher severity than the last alert always fires. That second part came from a bug: the first cooldown version hid a drawdown breach for 50 seconds after a clear. My first fix let every breach bypass the cooldown, which broke an existing test, and the final rule distinguishes severities. (D14, D14b)

The trade-off is false positives against missed breaches, and I made it a number. Clean sessions peak at 36 in absolute position (a later seed reached 37), so warn at 60 is 1.67 times that. Clean max drawdown is 77, warn at 100 is 1.3 times it. The caveat is that the clean margins come from the same simulator that made the bad session, so they do not prove a false-positive rate on real flow. (README, Alert design; D15)"

**The bad session (about 1.5 minutes).** "I stitched five simulated segments on one continuous grid with inventory carried over, so each alert can be checked in the phase that should cause it. The markout alert fires first at 1836 seconds, 36 seconds into the adverse phase. Position warns 43 seconds into the inventory run and breaches at 3139 seconds. The loss-from-peak breach fires at 3497 seconds. Fill-rate breaches 49 seconds into the drought. Nine alerts in all, in order. The clean session of the same seed raises none. (examples/demo_bad.txt, D16)"

**Server and UI (about 45 seconds).** "A standard-library web server and one HTML page, because I wanted a real screenshot in the README with no third-party UI dependency. It binds to 127.0.0.1 only, with no authentication, and I did not deploy it. (D09, D20)"

**Review and testing (about 1.5 minutes).** "123 tests: core 28, alert layer 61, CLI and server 20, regressions 14. An independent review found five issues: the cooldown hiding a higher severity, a mid-instant flush freezing an instant, the bad scenario dropping rows for some hour values, a fill-rate drought followed by a runaway raising no second alert, and limits that load but cannot behave. Each was reproduced by the reviewer and fixed with a regression test. (README, How it was checked; D17)"

**Performance and limits (about 45 seconds).** "The full engine processed 231,895 events in 2.1 seconds, about 110,000 per second. An agent had reported 420,000, which was only the state machine, so I quote my own measurement. (D26) Limits: thresholds only fit this simulator, loss-from-peak can stay in breach until equity recovers, and a rule never steps from breach down to warn. (README, Limitations)"

## Questions

**1. Is this a real edge?** No, there is no strategy claim at all. The market maker's sessions are near break-even and the data is simulated. The project demonstrates monitoring. (README; D04)

**2. What would you do with real data?** Feed it real fills and quotes in the same schema, then recalibrate every threshold from real clean sessions; the current numbers are fitted to the simulator and say nothing about real flow. I would also check the same-timestamp ordering convention against how the real feed stamps fills. (D11, D15)

**3. What did the review find?** Five alert-layer bugs, the notable one being the cooldown hiding a higher-severity breach after a clear. All were reproduced and fixed with regression tests. (D14b, D17)

**4. What would you do differently?** Calibrate on more than one generator, add step-down from breach to warn, and add authentication before anything beyond localhost. (README, Limitations; D20)

**5. Why hysteresis and a cooldown?** Without hysteresis a metric hovering at a threshold flaps. Without a cooldown a persistent condition spams. Both trade a little latency for far fewer false alerts. (docs/learning/hysteresis-and-cooldown.md; D14)

**6. How do you know the incremental numbers are right?** They are compared to Project 1's batch code, which was itself tested against an independent equity computation. The reviewer matched 72,000 ticks on a fresh seed. (tests/test_equivalence.py; D10)

# Project 3: Market-data normaliser and order-book rebuild (C++17)

## 2-minute script

"This is a C++17 tool that takes two venue feeds in different formats, turns them into one event schema, rebuilds the order book for each venue, and merges them into one quote stream that Project 1 can analyse. The feeds are synthetic. I tried to download real venue data and the sandbox refused, so I generated two modelled formats and I say so everywhere. No real venue was tested. (README, Read this first; D25)

Venue A is JSON lines with sequence ranges and absolute quantities. Venue B is CSV with chained ids and prices scaled by 10 to the 4th. Different conventions force the normaliser to do real work. Everything is fixed-point: int64 ticks and lots, no floating point. (D29, D33)

The part I care about is correctness under faults. If a sequence gap, duplicate or out-of-order message appears, the venue is marked stale, later updates are buffered, and the next snapshot resyncs it. The book is never published while it might be wrong. On a 15-minute feed with injected faults, each venue shows two gaps and three resyncs, and the rebuilt book equals the venue's checkpoint snapshots. (D30, README Results)

There are 85 C++ tests, passing in a normal build and an AddressSanitizer and UBSan build, with all warnings as errors. A one-command benchmark reports 2.02 million events per second end to end and a per-update latency of 71 nanoseconds at the median and 158 at the 99th percentile, on one shared machine, so treat them as orders of magnitude. (D35)

An independent review found three defects, including undefined behaviour on overflow, and I fixed all three with regression tests. (D43)"

## 10-minute script

**Opening (about 45 seconds).** "A market-data handler turns raw exchange messages into a trustworthy book. The risk is silent corruption: one missed message and your book is wrong, but nobody notices. This project is about the discipline to avoid that. Synthetic feeds, no real venue, two modelled formats. (README; D25)"

**Why synthetic, and the formats (about 1.5 minutes).** "I probed two public data hosts and the proxy refused both, so I did not write adapters. A parser for a real venue written without seeing a real file would be a guess. Instead the generator writes two feeds modelled on common conventions: sequence-ranged diffs, and chained-id deltas. Venue A is JSON lines, each depth diff has first_seq and last_seq and absolute quantities where zero deletes the level. Venue B is CSV with explicit add, change and delete actions, prev_id and id chained, nanosecond exchange time and prices scaled by 10^4. (DESIGN.md sections 2 and 3; D25, D29)"

**Pipeline (about 1.5 minutes).** "Parse, sequence, apply, merge, export. Parsers use integer arithmetic only; prices are int64 ticks, quantities int64 lots, times int64 nanoseconds. A session per venue checks continuity and owns the buffer. The book is an L2 book with strictly ordered levels and no stored zero quantities. The merger reads each live venue's top of book after every raw message, takes the best bid across venues with quantities summed at that price, and orders by local capture time only. I never compare exchange timestamps from different venues. (README, How it works; D33)"

**Gap policy (about 2 minutes).** "Continuity for A is first_seq at most last plus one, at most last_seq. For B, prev_id must equal the last id. A break marks the venue stale: it stops publishing, and later updates are buffered. The next snapshot resyncs: buffered updates at or before it are dropped, the rest applied in order. Duplicates are ignored and counted. An out-of-order message takes the gap path. A crossed or locked single-venue book is treated as corruption. (D30)

On the faulted 15-minute feed each venue shows gaps 2, out-of-order 1, duplicates ignored 1, crossed detected 1, resyncs 3, and zero checkpoint mismatches. One honest limit: the generated feeds send snapshots in-stream, so the branch that applies the rest of the buffered diffs after a snapshot is not reached by them. Hand-built unit tests cover it. (README, Limitations; tests/test_session.cpp)"

**Export to Project 1 (about 45 seconds).** "The merged quotes export as timestamp, bid, ask, mid, only when both sides exist and the merged book is not stale. The mid has three decimals, since a half-tick mid cannot be written exactly in two. Locked merged quotes occur legitimately because Venue B lags A, and I measured 1,017 of them on the seed-7 feed. Venue B's clock runs 300 microseconds ahead by construction. I document that rather than correct it. (D31, D32)"

**Testing and benchmark (about 1.5 minutes).** "85 C++ tests, 32 Python tests. Hand-rolled harness, no external dependencies. Both builds use -Werror with a strict warning set, and the sanitizer build is clean. Golden files are compared byte for byte. The benchmark discards a warm-up and takes the median of five runs: parse 3.95 million events per second, apply 23.1 million, end to end 2.02 million, update latency p50 71 nanoseconds and p99 158. That includes about 28 nanoseconds of timer cost I report but do not subtract, and throughput moved about 30 percent between runs. (D33, D35)"

**Review (about 1 minute).** "An independent review found three issues, each reproduced. One: the merge kept file order per feed, so out-of-order local timestamps made the export's time go backwards. Two: the benchmark exited with an error whenever a feed contained a parse error. Three: signed integer overflow, which is undefined behaviour, on extreme input values. I fixed them by sorting globally, counting parse errors consistently and saturating arithmetic. I built the original in a throwaway worktree and showed it failing on the same inputs. Nine regression tests were added, and a mutation check confirmed they fail without the fix. (D43)"

**Limits (about 20 seconds).** "Synthetic feeds only, one machine, no network I/O, trades are passed through not analysed, and no real venue format verified. (README, Limitations)"

## Questions

**1. Is this a real edge?** The question does not apply: there is no strategy here. It is infrastructure. The only trading content is the exported quotes, which are synthetic. (README)

**2. What would you do with real data?** Get one real captured feed, write a parser from the actual documented format, and run the same golden, gap and checkpoint tests against the venue's own snapshots. I would not trust the formats here to transfer. (D25)

**3. What did the review find?** Three defects: merge order with out-of-order local timestamps, benchmark counters failing on parse errors, and signed overflow on extreme values. Fixed, with nine regression tests. (D43)

**4. What would you do differently?** Make the generator send snapshots out of band so the buffered-straddle resync branch is exercised end to end, and report latency with the timer cost subtracted or measured with a lower-overhead method. (README, Limitations; D35)

**5. Why fixed-point?** Exact arithmetic for prices and quantities and no float comparison bugs in the book. (D33, docs/learning/fixed-point-prices.md)

**6. Are 71 ns and 2 million events per second realistic for production?** No claim of that. It is one shared machine, synthetic data, no network or disk, and about 30 percent run-to-run variation. Treat it as an order of magnitude. (D35)

# Project 4: Event-driven market-making backtester

## 2-minute script

"This project asks how much a backtest's fill assumptions change its PnL. I built a deterministic event-driven simulator in C++17 with order latency, cancels in flight and queue position. It reuses Project 3's order book and runs a passive market maker on a synthetic feed. Same inputs give byte-identical fills. (DESIGN.md; D36, D44)

There are three queue models. Optimistic: you fill whenever the market trades at or through your price. FIFO back-of-queue: you join the back of the displayed queue and only trades at your price move you forward. Cancel-ahead: some fraction of unexplained displayed-size drops count as ahead of you. (D39)

The study is 96 cells: four latencies, four queue models, the cross-fill rule on and off, three seeds, one hour each. Optimistic produced about twice the fills of back-of-queue with the rule on and about six times with it off, and about 21 currency units more PnL in both settings. (results/sensitivity_table.md, sections 1.7 and 2.7)

The surprise: with the default cross-fill rule on, about 76 percent of the back-of-queue fills came from that rule, and they hid the effect of latency. With it off, the one-second markout falls from plus 0.406 basis points at zero latency to plus 0.279 at 20 milliseconds. So I report both settings. (D45)

An independent review found no logic defects and three edge cases, now fixed. There are 67 C++ tests and 17 Python tests. (D46)

Everything is synthetic. The direction is the finding; the magnitudes belong to the generator. A backtest is not live PnL. (results/sensitivity_table.md, Statement)"

## 10-minute script

**Opening (about 45 seconds).** "Project 1 had a toy fill model. Real backtests overstate PnL when they assume fills they would not have got. I built a simulator to measure how much, and I will show the design, the study, a result I did not expect, and the limits. All synthetic, and a backtest is not live PnL. (roadmap section 6)"

**Design (about 2.5 minutes).** "Project 4 links Project 3's book code and reads the normalised events file that Project 3 already writes. Its market file must be byte-identical to Project 3's export for Venue A, and an integration test checks it. One book implementation to trust. (D36)

Event ordering is deterministic. At equal timestamps market events go first, then exchange arrivals, then reports, then timers. Orders and cancels share one first-in-first-out channel so a cancel cannot overtake the order it cancels. (D37) A single latency L applies to order entry, cancel and each ack direction, with optional seeded jitter, so the sensitivity study has one knob. (D38) Cancels in flight matter: an order can still fill while your cancel is travelling, and the simulator counts those. Orders are post-only: one that would cross on arrival is rejected. (D40)

Queue models are pure functions in integer lots. Optimistic fills when the market trades at or through your price. FIFO back-of-queue joins the back of the displayed queue and only trades at your price advance it. Cancel-ahead with fraction f counts a share f of unexplained size drops as ahead of you; with f equal to zero it equals back-of-queue. Queue ahead is capped at displayed size, which is arithmetic, not an assumption. (D39)

Output is in Project 1's fills schema, so Project 1 analyses it. A determinism hash, FNV-1a over the bytes of fills.csv, makes reproducibility checkable. (D42, D44)"

**What is and is not identifiable (about 1 minute).** "I tested what the synthetic feed lets me say. In the generator every trade is followed by an exact one-level reduction, so trades and cancels are perfectly separable, which would not hold in real data. Non-trade decreases at the touch outnumber trades, so the cancel-ahead fraction f cannot be identified from the data. The study shows direction and rough size of the assumption effects in this simulator, not what f is and not magnitudes for a real venue. (D42)"

**The study and results (about 2.5 minutes).** "96 cells: latencies 0, 1, 5 and 20 milliseconds, four queue models, cross-fill rule on and off, seeds 7, 8, 9, one hour each, about 9 seconds of wall time. (results/run_info.txt, sensitivity_table.md)

With the rule on at zero latency, optimistic gets 19,284 fills against 9,635 for back-of-queue, about 2.0 times, and total PnL of minus 20.86 against minus 42.74. With the rule off, optimistic gets 12,025 fills against 1,993, about 6.0 times, and PnL plus 9.71 against minus 11.31. The gap in total PnL is about 21 currency units in both. That is the inflation: a simple 'we fill when the market trades through' assumption turns a losing market maker into a winning one on this synthetic market. (sections 1.4, 1.7, 2.4, 2.7)

The unexpected part. At first the rule-on study showed latency barely changing anything and strongly negative spread capture of about minus 27 to minus 30 for every FIFO model. That looked wrong, so I ran the design's ablation switch. About 7,500 of FIFO_BACK's 9,622 fills on seed 7 came from the cross-fill rule, which fills a resting order once at your price when the market moves onto it, regardless of queue position. They were stale quotes picked off by price moves. With the rule off, back-of-queue has 2,087 fills on seed 7, positive spread capture, and latency matters: the one-second markout falls from plus 0.406 basis points to plus 0.279 at 20 milliseconds on seed means, and spread capture from 5.73 to 4.51. So with the rule on, the latency effect is hidden, not absent. I kept the default unchanged and report both settings. (D45, sections 1.5, 2.2, 2.3)"

**Review (about 1.5 minutes).** "An independent review of the core found no defects in the queue models, cancels in flight, the order channel, event ordering, post-only, cross fills, bookkeeping or determinism, after 5,000 fuzz iterations under sanitizers and a 500-feed model-ordering fuzz. It found three edge cases. A schedule period of zero re-armed a timer at the same instant forever and exhausted memory. Huge latency values overflowed signed int64. Two test checks could not fail. All fixed; I reran the full study and the output is byte-identical. 67 C++ tests in both builds, 17 Python tests. (D46)"

**What I will and will not claim (about 1 minute).** "The direction of the effect, that optimistic fill assumptions raise fills and backtest PnL, is the finding. The magnitudes are properties of the generator. The ordering of models for the market maker is reported, not asserted, because its fills change its later orders. (D41) A backtest cannot reproduce how other participants react or real latency distributions. Senior HFT postings ask for a live track record and no project here substitutes for one. (results/sensitivity_table.md, Statement; firm-landscape.md)"

## Questions

**1. Is this a real edge?** No. Even the optimistic model's positive PnL is an artefact of its own assumption on synthetic data; the realistic models lose money. It is evidence about backtest inflation, not about a strategy. (sections 1.1, 2.1; D04)

**2. What would you do with real data?** Run the same study on captured depth data and, if I had the venue's own order-level data, estimate the fraction of size drops that are cancels ahead of me instead of assuming it. The synthetic feed cannot identify that fraction. (D42)

**3. What did the review find?** No logic defects, and three edge cases: a zero-period timer that never advanced time, signed overflow on huge latency, and two assertions that could not fail. Fixed, with results unchanged. (D46)

**4. What would you do differently?** Separate entry and cancel latencies, choose the cross-fill default more carefully (I kept the design's choice and disclosed it), and test on a feed where trades and cancels are not perfectly separable. (D38, D42, D45)

**5. Why is the cross-fill finding important?** It shows how a single modelling default can make a latency study report 'latency does not matter'. Showing both settings is the honest presentation. (D45)

**6. How do you know the runs are reproducible?** The same inputs give byte-identical fills, checked by a hash, and the full 96-cell study rerun after the review fixes was byte-identical. (D44, D46)
